# Onboarding is a retention problem, not a UX problem

**Marcus Feld:** [00:01:05] Most teams treat onboarding as a funnel to optimise.
Reduce the steps, cut the fields, add a progress bar. Those are fine. They are
also why most onboarding rewrites move activation by two points and then stall.

**Marcus Feld:** [00:03:22] The thing that actually moves activation is finding
the one action that correlates with week-four retention and making the entire
first session about that action. For us it was inviting a second person. Not
because collaboration was our positioning — it was not — but because the data
said a solo account never survived month two.

**Lenny:** How did you find that action?

**Marcus Feld:** [00:05:40] Boring regression on a year of cohort data. We listed
every event in the first seven days, then looked at which ones separated the
retained cohort from the churned one. Eleven candidates, one that held up when we
controlled for company size. Teams skip this and pick the action they wish
mattered.

**Marcus Feld:** [00:08:15] Then we did the unpopular part. We removed four
things from onboarding that users liked, because they delayed the invite step.
Satisfaction scores in the first session went down. Week-four retention went up
nine points. If your onboarding metric is how the first session feels, you will
optimise for the wrong thing every time.

**Marcus Feld:** [00:11:30] Time to value is the phrase everyone uses and almost
nobody defines. Define it as a specific event with a specific timestamp, or it is
a slogan. Ours was "second user accepts invite within 24 hours". You can argue
with that definition. You cannot argue about whether it happened.
